# P07 Benin youth self-employment: arm-specific persistence and cost evidence

**Status:** producer research note; requires independent verification before decision-bearing integration  
**Problem:** P07 — Economic Opportunity and Meaningful Work  
**Primary source:** World Bank, *Final evaluation report of Benin’s Youth Employment project* (June 2022), https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099005006302210770/pdf/P1477800853c6e050ba2403b0a1ba2239d.pdf

## Decision question

Does the Benin experiment supply the arm-specific persistence and cost information needed to distinguish business/life-skills training from a cash grant and from their bundle, without reopening a global active-labour-market-program ranking?

## Design and timing

The evaluation randomized 3,444 eligible applicants equally across four groups: training + cash grant (T1), training only (T2), cash grant only (T3), and control. The report’s explicit timing table places follow-ups about 15, 27, and 45 months after training and 8, 20, and 38 months after grant delivery. The third follow-up was phone-based; response fell to 83.4%, with significantly higher attrition in the grant-containing T1 and T3 arms. The authors report robustness checks using inverse-probability weighting, but this remains a material long-run uncertainty.

**Source-location caution:** prose introducing the results later reverses the 38/45-month labels. Because Table 3 explicitly maps each intervention to each follow-up and the intervention sequence places training before grant delivery, this note uses Table 3’s 45-month-after-training / 38-month-after-grant chronology and records the internal inconsistency rather than silently harmonizing it.

## Arm-specific findings

### T2 — training only

At the long follow-up, the report finds significant positive business-performance effects for both women and men. Monthly earnings were about **16,000 FCFA (US$28.8 in July 2021) higher for men** and **9,000 FCFA (US$16.2) higher for women**. The report states that training effects grew over the three follow-ups rather than attenuating and that business practices improved in the training arms by the earlier follow-ups.

The reported **training cost is US$1,050 per beneficiary**. The cost definition is unusually useful: the report says per-capita component costs include prorated project management, sensitization/communication, enrollment operations, staff/equipment, lotteries, monitoring/supervision, and training design/trainer-training/delivery. Its own simple earnings-based calculation says the training cost is recouped after about **36 months for men** and **64 months for women**; the women’s figure explicitly assumes the observed earnings impact persists beyond the last follow-up.

This is a source-reported payback heuristic, not a full cost-effectiveness estimate: it does not discount future earnings, value non-earnings outcomes, account for taxes/transfers or general-equilibrium effects, or establish portability to another training design or labour market.

### T3 — cash grant only

The grant component cost **US$629 per beneficiary**. At the long follow-up, the grant-only arm had **no significant effect on revenues, profits, or earnings for either women or men** and significantly underperformed training on business performance. The report also describes strong short-run negative effects on women’s business performance and earnings that attenuated over time.

The grant-only arm nevertheless reduced women’s insufficient employment by about **5 percentage points** at the long follow-up and produced some non-earnings welfare changes. That prevents a blanket claim that the grant had “no benefit”; it is more accurate to say the grant did not produce the persistent earnings/business-performance gains seen in T2 and had heterogeneous effects across outcomes and gender.

### T1 — training + grant

Adding the grant to training did **not** produce a clean dominance result. Long-run business-performance impacts were much smaller than T2 and no longer significant; T1 and T2 differed significantly for women, while equality could not be rejected for men. The grant-containing bundle did increase capital stock, and women assigned T1 had about **0.36 additional paid employees** at the long follow-up, so a narrow earnings-only ranking would discard potentially relevant employment and asset effects.

The report’s component costs imply a mechanical combined component total of **US$1,679 per beneficiary** ($1,050 training + $629 grant), but the report does not present that sum as a formal T1 cost-effectiveness ratio. It should therefore be treated only as a transparent arithmetic component total, subject to the report’s cost-allocation method.

## Decision delta

The Benin case materially narrows the P07 comparison:

1. **Do not pool training, grant, and bundle as one entrepreneurship-support modality.** Their long-run outcome profiles differ sharply.
2. **Persistence changes the training judgment.** The report says T2 gains increased over time, and the source-reported payback calculation depends on a horizon substantially longer than the short follow-ups common in this literature.
3. **Cash alone is not supported as an earnings/business-performance substitute for this training in this population.** Its long-run monetary business outcomes were null while some employment/welfare outcomes moved differently.
4. **The bundle is not automatically better because it contains more inputs.** T1 failed to dominate T2 on the main business-performance/earnings evidence and is mechanically more expensive, although it produced distinct capital and paid-employment effects.
5. **Do not export a scalar ranking.** The experiment is one context with a gender-informed, locally delivered, closely supervised training package among applicants with substantial prior technical/apprenticeship exposure; cost and effect portability are unresolved.

## Remaining uncertainty / next information-value gap

The highest-value follow-up is no longer “find a Benin cost.” The report supplies component costs and a source-reported training payback calculation. The remaining gap is **comparability**: identify another intervention with a similarly inclusive cost denominator, a compatible earnings/employment outcome, and a sufficiently long horizon, or explicitly conclude that no defensible cross-study scalar comparison is available. Any future comparison should preserve arm, gender, outcome family, horizon, and cost-definition differences rather than converting these numbers into a portable global ALMP score.
