## 1. Bosnia: focused missing-denominator search

### What the primary paper does establish

Balavac-Orlic, Giles, Hari & Ovadiya (World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 10826, 2024) documents that:

- the private provider was selected competitively based on employment-service capability and employer network;
- the provider received a payment for each Individual Action Plan (IAP) developed;
- an additional payment was made for each beneficiary who secured formal employment, whether by own search or provider matching;
- payment eligibility required verified formal employment for at least three consecutive months;
- the provider faced a target of helping 500 jobseekers find sustained employment, with part of payment contingent on sustained employment;
- specific matching was considered costly to the provider and was disproportionately allocated to jobseekers assessed as more employable;
- the authors explicitly warn that contracting and monitoring have sizeable transaction costs and that scale-up may require a tiered payment structure to avoid cream-skimming.

These details are sufficient to reason about provider incentives, but not to calculate incremental program cost.

### What the companion policy note adds

The World Bank policy note, *Private Provisioning of Employment Services in Bosnia and Herzegovina*, confirms that payment requests for successful placements were independently verified by the Federal Employment Institute and that short-run employment measurement corresponds to the payment-verification window. It does not expose the payment amounts in the public text reviewed here.

### What broader project-budget material does **not** establish

World Bank trust-fund records for *Developing Private Employment Services in Bosnia and Herzegovina* report program-level contributions/disbursements. A 2018 directory reports roughly US$0.60 million in contributions paid in and US$0.09 million disbursed by end-June 2018; a separate Knowledge for Change research proposal lists US$90,000 of research disbursement projections. These figures cover broader operational/research activity and predate the final 2020–2021 pilot implementation window. They are **not** a defensible estimate of:

- provider contract value;
- payment per IAP;
- payment per sustained placement;
- incremental matching cost above counseling;
- cost per assigned treatment participant; or
- cost per additional formal job caused by treatment.

Using any of these broader budgets as the missing denominator would create a false cost-effectiveness comparison.

### Bounded negative result

After a focused search of the World Bank working paper, companion policy note, trust-fund records, and project/research identifiers, this pass did **not** find a public primary source exposing the Bosnia provider's payment amounts or realized incremental matching cost.

This is a useful negative result: the current P07 framework should retain Bosnia's cost cell as **missing / not publicly recovered**, not estimate it from project totals.

## 2. Benin: arm-specific persistence and cost-effectiveness signal

The World Bank evaluation report *Addressing capital and skills constraints to youth self-employment in Benin* randomizes 3,444 eligible applicants across four equal groups:

- **T1:** business/life-skills training + cash grant;
- **T2:** training only;
- **T3:** cash grant only;
- **Control:** neither component.

The report identifies follow-up windows of approximately **15, 27, and 45 months after training**, corresponding to approximately **8, 20, and 38 months after grant delivery**. The third follow-up therefore provides a substantially longer persistence check than the first post-program snapshot.

### Training-only (T2)

At the third follow-up, training-only has strong positive and statistically significant effects on the synthetic business-performance measure for both men and women. Profits, revenues, and total earnings show the same direction. The report gives monthly earnings increases of approximately:

- **16,000 FCFA (US$28.8) for men**;
- **9,000 FCFA (US$16.2) for women**.

The report states that training effects grow rather than attenuate across follow-ups for key business outcomes. It also reports a per-capita training cost of about **US$1,050**, including allocated project-management, enrollment, monitoring, supervision, training-design, trainer-training, and delivery costs.

The report's own simple payback calculation says the training cost would be recouped through the observed earnings increment after roughly **36 months for men** and **64 months for women**, with the female calculation explicitly relying on sustained effects after the last observed follow-up.

### Grant-only (T3)

Grant-only has **no significant impact on revenues, profits, or earnings for either women or men at the third follow-up**. The report also finds strong short-run negative impacts of grant-only on women's business performance, including profits, revenues, and earnings, which attenuate over time. Men do not show the same short-run negative pattern.

Grant-only does reduce women's insufficient employment by about **5 percentage points** at the long follow-up, so the arm should not be summarized as uniformly harmful or useless. It also affects expenditures and business ownership/assets in some dimensions. The key decision point is narrower: **grant-only does not supply the sustained earnings/business-performance result needed to justify treating it as equivalent to training-only.**

The reported per-capita grant-component cost is about **US$629**, which includes more than the transfer face value (project management, enrollment, monitoring, grant delivery/payment fees, and the grants themselves).

### Training + grant (T1)

The combination arm does not mechanically dominate training-only. For women, the training-plus-grant business-performance impact is materially smaller than training-only and no longer significant at the third follow-up; coefficient-equality tests distinguish T1 from T2 for women. For men there are some positive T1 signals, including revenues at the 10% level and total earnings, but the report notes that these findings are less robust than the T2 pattern.

The combination does have distinct benefits on some outcomes: for example, women in T1 have roughly 0.36 additional paid employees at the long follow-up, approximately one paid job for every three treated women. T1 also raises productive capital stock. These outcomes matter, but they are different objectives from participant earnings and therefore should not be collapsed into one scalar “entrepreneurship effectiveness” score.

## 3. Updated P07 routing implication

The canonical binding-constraint framework should retain the following component-level rule pending C verification:

1. **Information/referral constraint + suitable vacancies + employer willingness + worker acceptance:** Bosnia-style matching remains a plausible route, but cost-effectiveness is unresolved until a real incremental cost denominator is recovered.
2. **Skills/business-practice constraint among participants with viable trades/self-employment:** Benin training-only has a sustained earnings/business-performance signal over roughly 45 months after training.
3. **Capital alone:** Benin grant-only should not be assumed to improve participant earnings or business performance; its welfare/employment effects are outcome- and gender-specific.
4. **Training + capital bundle:** evaluate separately rather than assuming additivity; the Benin combination underperforms training-only on some female business outcomes while improving some employment-creation/capital outcomes.
5. **Scalar comparison across these studies:** remain stopped until costs, outcome definitions, treatment architecture, and horizons are denominator-compatible.
