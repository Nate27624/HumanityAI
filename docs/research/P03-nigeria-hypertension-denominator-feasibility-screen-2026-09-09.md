# P03 Nigeria hypertension denominator-feasibility screen

**Reviewed:** 2026-09-09  
**Producer:** Operator A  
**Controller lineage:** Worker D assigned a cross-problem decision-synthesis path; the latest integrated synthesis then allocated one P03 denominator-feasibility screen before any second P03 producer slot.

## Decision question

Does Nigeria currently expose **two realistically available hypertension delivery packages** that can be compared on a common blood-pressure-control definition, horizon, cost perspective, medication availability, workforce burden, and retention denominator well enough to justify a second P03 package-comparison slot?

## Short answer

**Not yet. Do not release the second P03 comparison slot on the current public evidence.**

Nigeria is unusually decision-rich for hypertension delivery because the HEARTS-adapted Hypertension Treatment in Nigeria (HTN) program has public implementation, retention, medication-stock, workforce, and costing evidence. However, the strongest clinical-outcome series and the strongest HEARTS-vs-usual-care costing comparison do not line up cleanly enough by geography, comparator, and outcome denominator to support a defensible incremental cost-per-controlled-patient comparison.

The useful next step is narrower: recover **state-matched clinical control/retention outcomes for Kano and/or Ogun usual care versus HEARTS**, or explicitly compare within-HEARTS implementation variants on a shared denominator. Until then, Nigeria supports component-level optimization decisions but not a clean two-package cost-effectiveness rank.

## Evidence recovered

### 1. The FCT HTN program provides a strong delivery-outcome signal

The HTN program adapted WHO HEARTS across **60 primary health-care centers in Nigeria's Federal Capital Territory** from 2020 through 2023. A 2026 implementation-outcomes report describes 21,922 enrolled patients and shows large implementation changes, including fixed-dose-combination prescribing rising from 16.3% to 65.2%, medication availability after a drug revolving fund, and improved six-month retention.

Key implementation outcomes reported for the program include:

- **21,922 patients** recruited across 60 PHCs, most of them rural;
- **95% of PHCs** had at least one 30-day supply of a blood-pressure-lowering medicine in stock after the drug revolving fund was implemented;
- **six-month retention increased from 59.9% to 63.1%** between pre-implementation and implementation periods;
- later impact reporting describes hypertension treatment increasing from about 86.8% at baseline to 96.2% at study end and control increasing from about 22.3% to 56.4%.

Sources:

- Ye et al., *Implementation outcomes from the Hypertension Treatment in Nigeria program: results from a type 2 hybrid interrupted time series trial*, Implementation Science (2026), PubMed PMID 41299526: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41299526/
- Ojji et al. / HTN program implementation study, *Implementation of a large-scale hypertension program in primary health centres in the Federal Capital Territory, Nigeria*, BMJ Open 15(7), 2025: https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/15/7/e103121
- HTN program impact evaluation, PubMed PMID 42534330: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42534330/

**Decision implication:** Nigeria clearly passes the test for a measurable delivery cascade: medication continuity, treatment, retention, and control are observable rather than inferred from screening volume.

### 2. Nigeria also has unusually concrete cost and workforce evidence

A 2025 cost analysis used the HEARTS costing tool on 15 sampled primary-care facilities in **Kano, Ogun, and FCT Abuja**, from a health-system perspective. It estimated the annual cost of HEARTS at roughly **US$16 per adult primary-care user** overall, with location-specific estimates of approximately US$21 in Abuja, US$11 in Kano, and US$16 in Ogun.

The same study estimated annual medication costs per treated HEARTS patient at roughly **US$28 in Abuja, US$16 in Kano, and US$27 in Ogun**. For usual care, medication costs were approximately US$32 in Kano and US$16 in Ogun. Provider time and medication were the dominant HEARTS cost components. The modeled full-scale staffing requirement was about **0.45 physician, 1.59 nurse, and 5.21 community-health-worker FTEs per 10,000 primary-care users**.

The analysis also estimated that task sharing from physicians toward nurses and community health workers would reduce modeled personnel costs, and it identifies visit spacing, multi-month prescriptions, protocol adherence, and medicine purchasing as plausible cost levers.

Source:

- *Analysis of costs in implementing the HEARTS hypertension program in Nigerian primary care*, Cost Effectiveness and Resource Allocation (2025), PubMed PMID 40426221: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40426221/
- Full open-access article: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12117767/

**Decision implication:** Nigeria passes the cost-perspective and workforce-input screen better than many candidate LMIC systems. It is suitable for bounded implementation optimization.

### 3. The medicine/procurement mechanism is observable but not frictionless

The FCT implementation literature attributes improved medicine availability partly to a **drug revolving fund** and reports minimal stockouts after implementation, while also preserving patient-affordability concerns. The costing study shows meaningful cross-state differences in medicine mix and cost: Kano's HEARTS prescribing relied heavily on inexpensive first-line amlodipine, while Ogun had more patients on higher protocol steps and higher average medication cost.

The costing paper also notes that usual-care prescribing in Kano and Ogun was not standardized and that medicine availability appears to have constrained prescribing patterns, including heavy methyldopa use in usual care.

**Decision implication:** drug procurement, protocol adherence, and patient affordability are not background details; they are candidate binding constraints that can change both program cost and control yield.

## Why the two-package comparison still fails

The public evidence does **not** yet satisfy the common-denominator test required by the integrated cross-problem allocation note.

### Comparator mismatch

The strongest outcome trajectory is for the HTN program in **FCT Abuja**, whereas the most explicit HEARTS-versus-usual-care cost comparisons are reported for **Kano and Ogun**. Abuja did not have a comparable usual-care hypertension-treatment cost baseline in the costing analysis because participating facilities did not provide hypertension treatment before the HEARTS initiative.

### Outcome/cost alignment mismatch

The costing study explicitly says clinical outcome monitoring is still needed to establish cost-effectiveness versus usual care. It provides annual program and medicine costs, but those headline estimates cannot be combined mechanically with FCT control improvements to estimate an incremental cost per controlled patient in Kano or Ogun.

### Design mismatch

The HTN outcome evidence is based on an interrupted time-series / hybrid implementation design rather than a contemporaneous randomized package comparison. Even if the control definition is stable within the series, secular changes and implementation-period effects cannot be assumed equivalent to a clean two-package causal contrast.

### Horizon mismatch risk

The costing model annualizes resource use, while clinical retention and control are reported at implementation-specific horizons. A valid package comparison would need a shared time window and explicit handling of patients lost to follow-up.

## Decision rule

**Do not release the held second P03 producer slot for a full package cost-effectiveness comparison yet.**

Release it only if a follow-up screen can recover, for Kano or Ogun (or another Nigerian setting with equivalent cost data):

1. HEARTS and comparator blood-pressure control measured with the same threshold;
2. matched outcome horizon;
3. matched retention/loss-to-follow-up denominator;
4. health-system costs for both packages over that horizon;
5. medicine availability and patient-price assumptions; and
6. implementation differences sufficient to define two real package alternatives rather than a before/after label.

If those data remain unavailable, the higher-value Nigeria question is **within-HEARTS component optimization**, especially whether visit spacing, multi-month prescriptions, task sharing, or procurement changes reduce cost without degrading retention and control.

## START / MORE / LESS / STOP

**START:** search specifically for state-matched Kano/Ogun clinical control and retention outcomes tied to the same HEARTS/usual-care facilities or implementation periods used in the costing analysis.

**MORE:** component-level evidence on visit frequency, multi-month dispensing, medicine purchasing, patient affordability, and task-sharing effects on retained controlled patients.

**LESS:** broad Nigeria hypertension burden summaries; the system-level delivery bottleneck is already established.

**STOP:** combining Abuja control gains with Kano/Ogun cost deltas as if they were one causal cost-effectiveness comparison; treating lower medication cost alone as evidence of a better package; or computing a national cost-per-controlled-patient scalar from unmatched sources.

## Decision delta for Worker D

The previous cross-problem synthesis conditionally favored P03 because Nigeria-like systems might expose two commensurable delivery packages. This screen narrows that expectation:

- **Nigeria is highly useful for implementation and component-cost analysis.**
- **Nigeria does not yet earn a second P03 package-comparison slot on the evidence recovered here.**
- The next P03 slot should be released only for targeted denominator recovery, not for a full package rank.
- If matched Kano/Ogun control-retention data cannot be recovered quickly, reallocate the held slot rather than extending a low-yield search.

**Confidence:** high that the current public sources are insufficient for a clean HEARTS-versus-usual-care incremental cost-per-controlled-patient comparison; moderate-high that Nigeria remains a strong setting for bounded within-program cost/adoption optimization; moderate that state-matched outcomes can be recovered with one further targeted search.
