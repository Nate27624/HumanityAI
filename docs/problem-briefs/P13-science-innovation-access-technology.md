# P13 — Science, innovation, and access to technology

## Why this problem matters

Scientific knowledge and useful technology expand what people can understand, build, treat, communicate, and choose. P13 includes both the production of knowledge and the ability to benefit from it: research capacity, open access, translation of discoveries into practice, digital connectivity, affordability, skills, devices, accessibility, and unequal access to frontier technologies. A connectivity count is therefore only one slice of the problem.

## Current baseline

Canonical indicator [`I0013`](../../data/indicators.json) records the International Telecommunication Union's 2025 estimate that **2.2 billion people remained offline**, while about 6.0 billion people, or 74% of the world's population, were using the Internet.

This is a threshold access/use measure, not a measure of meaningful technological capability. ITU's 2025 reporting also emphasizes continuing differences in quality and affordability, and large divides by income and geography. The canonical record therefore should not be read as saying that everyone counted as online has reliable, affordable, safe, accessible, or productive connectivity.

Source: ITU, *Measuring digital development: Facts and Figures 2025* (17 November 2025).

## Existing reusable infrastructure

Canonical resource [`RSC0006`](../../data/resources.json) maps the **UNESCO Open Science Platform**, a public gateway to publications, reports, policy papers, and dashboards produced or supported by UNESCO's Natural Sciences Family.

Its relevance is discovery and reuse of existing open scientific knowledge. Its presence is not evidence that open-science policies are effective, that the platform is comprehensive, or that scientific capacity is equally distributed. HumanityAI should use such infrastructure before duplicating it while separately evaluating outcomes.

## Decision-useful distinctions

P13 analysis should keep at least these dimensions separate:

1. **Connectivity:** Can people reach digital networks at all?
2. **Meaningful access:** Are connections affordable, reliable, fast enough, accessible, and paired with suitable devices?
3. **Skills and agency:** Can people use technology effectively and safely rather than merely be connected?
4. **Knowledge production:** Where are researchers, laboratories, funding, compute, data, and scientific institutions concentrated?
5. **Knowledge access:** Are publications, data, educational materials, standards, and software available to those who could use them?
6. **Translation and diffusion:** Do discoveries become useful products, services, practices, and public goods across settings?
7. **Distribution and control:** Who captures the benefits, who bears risks, and whether access depends on concentrated gatekeepers or coercive surveillance.

These dimensions can move independently. More Internet users do not necessarily imply more scientific capacity, and more research output does not necessarily imply broader human benefit.

## What current evidence does not establish

Current HumanityAI records do not yet establish:

- a multidimensional global baseline for meaningful connectivity beyond the offline population count;
- global disparities in scientific workforce, R&D funding, research infrastructure, compute, laboratory capacity, publication access, or technology diffusion;
- causal effects of open-access mandates, research-funding models, science-policy reforms, digital-skills programs, broadband subsidies, device programs, or other P13 interventions;
- whether specific innovation policies increase socially valuable innovation rather than only patents, publications, firm formation, or spending;
- comparative cost-effectiveness of connectivity, open-science, research-capacity, or technology-access interventions;
- HumanityAI's comparative advantage in implementing any such intervention.

## High-value next evidence

- Add complementary global indicators for affordability, connection quality, devices, digital skills, scientific workforce, R&D capacity, and access to research where definitions are comparable.
- Add systematic reviews and strong causal evidence on digital-access programs, research funding, open science, technology diffusion, and science-capacity interventions, preserving null and heterogeneous findings.
- Distinguish research inputs and outputs from downstream welfare, health, productivity, capability, and distributional outcomes.
- Map reusable datasets, repositories, standards, open-source tools, and scientific infrastructure before proposing parallel systems.
- Track who is excluded from new technologies by cost, disability, language, geography, legal restrictions, or institutional concentration.

## Rights and distribution cautions

Expanding technology access can increase capability while also increasing surveillance, manipulation, dependency, environmental burdens, cybersecurity exposure, or concentration of power. HumanityAI should treat privacy, accessibility, interoperability, user control, competition, and the ability to exit a technology ecosystem as part of the outcome rather than side constraints.

## What this brief does not establish

This brief does not claim that Internet access is equivalent to scientific or technological capability, that open science automatically improves outcomes, or that maximizing connectivity, publications, patents, compute, or technology adoption is an adequate objective. It summarizes the current canonical baseline and resource map and makes the most important evidence gaps explicit.
