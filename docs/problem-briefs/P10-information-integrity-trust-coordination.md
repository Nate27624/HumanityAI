# P10 — Information integrity, trust, and coordination

## Why this problem matters

Human cooperation depends on people being able to distinguish reliable from unreliable information, evaluate institutions, understand disagreement, and coordinate despite uncertainty. P10 therefore spans information quality, media literacy, institutional trust, polarization, propaganda, scientific communication, and collective-action failures. No single trust or misinformation metric can represent the whole problem.

## Current baseline

Canonical indicator [`I0015`](../../data/indicators.json) records the OECD's 2025 Trust Survey result that **40% of respondents across participating OECD countries reported high or moderately high trust in national government**, compared with 43% reporting low or no trust. The survey covers 33 OECD countries and five accession candidates.

This is an institutional-trust measure, not a global estimate of coordination quality. Trust can be warranted or unwarranted, and low trust can sometimes reflect justified scrutiny or poor institutional performance rather than a social pathology.

Source: OECD, *Survey on Drivers of Trust in Public Institutions 2026 Results* (29 June 2026).

Canonical indicator [`I0018`](../../data/indicators.json) adds a different dimension. In the 2024 OECD Truth Quest Survey, respondents across 21 countries correctly classified the veracity of sampled online content **60% of the time**. The survey included 40,765 participants and used a social-media-like interface.

This does not measure the prevalence of misinformation, real-world exposure, platform incentives, censorship, media pluralism, or whether people make good collective decisions. It is one cross-national measure of content-veracity identification skill.

Source: OECD, *The OECD Truth Quest Survey: Methodology and findings* (2024).

## Decision-useful distinctions

P10 analysis should keep at least these questions separate:

1. **Information supply:** How much false, misleading, low-quality, or manipulated content exists and how widely does it spread?
2. **Information evaluation:** Can people identify reliability, uncertainty, provenance, and manipulation?
3. **Institutional trust:** Which institutions are trusted, by whom, and is that trust supported by performance and accountability?
4. **Interpersonal and group trust:** Can people cooperate across social, political, ethnic, religious, or geographic divisions?
5. **Voice and legitimacy:** Do people believe they can influence decisions and that institutions respond fairly?
6. **Coordination outcomes:** Does a society actually solve collective problems, or merely report high trust or agreement?

These dimensions can move in different directions. Higher trust is not automatically better, and skepticism is not automatically dysfunction.

## What current evidence does not establish

Current HumanityAI records do not yet establish:

- a global P10 baseline beyond the present OECD-heavy coverage;
- the prevalence or reach of misinformation and disinformation across major information environments;
- whether media-literacy interventions produce durable improvements in real-world judgment;
- whether fact-checking, platform labeling, prebunking, moderation, transparency rules, or other interventions improve downstream outcomes without unacceptable side effects;
- how polarization, censorship, media concentration, algorithmic incentives, and institutional performance interact causally with trust;
- comparative cost-effectiveness of information-integrity or civic-coordination interventions;
- HumanityAI's comparative advantage in implementing any such intervention.

## High-value next evidence

- Add more globally representative measures of institutional trust, interpersonal trust, media freedom/pluralism, and information access where definitions are defensible.
- Add systematic reviews and randomized or strong quasi-experimental evidence on prebunking, fact-checking, media literacy, platform labels, deliberative processes, and related interventions, preserving null and adverse findings.
- Separate short-term belief correction from durable behavior change, institutional legitimacy, polarization, and collective-action outcomes.
- Measure distributional and rights effects: anti-misinformation policies can reduce harm but can also create censorship, surveillance, viewpoint discrimination, or concentrated gatekeeping power.
- Add preregistered forecasts only when clear outcome definitions and authoritative resolution sources can be frozen in advance.

## Rights and governance cautions

Information-integrity interventions sit close to freedom of expression and political power. HumanityAI should not infer that suppressing contested claims is justified merely because a government, platform, or model labels them false. Provenance, transparency, pluralism, due process, appeal mechanisms, independent scrutiny, and protection against concentrated control are part of the outcome, not secondary concerns.

## What this brief does not establish

This brief does not claim that low trust is inherently bad, that high trust is inherently good, that content-classification skill directly predicts real-world behavior, or that a specific moderation, fact-checking, regulatory, or civic intervention should be adopted. It summarizes current canonical records and identifies evidence needed for stronger decisions.
