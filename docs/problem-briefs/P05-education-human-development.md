# P05 — Education, knowledge, and human development

**Status:** early synthesis, reviewed 2026-09-07  
**Problem definition:** access to education, literacy, skill development, scientific knowledge, lifelong learning, and barriers preventing people from developing their abilities.

This brief summarizes current HumanityAI records. It is navigation and synthesis, not independent evidence, a priority ranking, or a policy recommendation.

## What the current evidence says

### Access remains a large global constraint

HumanityAI indicator [`I0008`](../../data/indicators.json) records UNESCO's estimate that **273 million children and youth were out of school in 2024**. UNESCO's 2026 Global Education Monitoring Report also reports that the global out-of-school population had risen for seven consecutive years.

That measure captures access/enrollment, not learning quality, attendance consistency, completion, literacy, skills acquired, safety, inclusion, or whether schooling expands meaningful human capability. Conflict-affected settings can also be systematically undercounted.

Source: UNESCO Global Education Monitoring Report, *2026 GEM Report — Access and equity: countdown to 2030*: https://www.unesco.org/reports/gem-report/en/2026

### Educational participation and educational outcomes are different

Several current HumanityAI evidence records caution against treating enrollment, program participation, or time spent studying as proof of learning gains.

- [`E0003`](../../data/evidence.json): a systematic review of off-grid electrification in low- and middle-income countries found a possible increase in study time, but no significant effects on school attendance or test scores. Publication bias may overstate the study-time effect.
- [`E0008`](../../data/evidence.json): centre-based early-education programmes for children roughly ages three to seven may make little to no difference in school readiness compared with no programme or standard curriculum; certainty varies by outcome and study quality.
- [`E0009`](../../data/evidence.json): summer education programmes for disadvantaged or at-risk young people show mixed effects, with some positive education outcomes but reading and mathematics test scores often showing no effect.

These findings do not establish that the interventions are useless. They show that intermediate activity measures and broad program labels are not enough to infer durable learning or capability gains.

### Evidence for youth-development programmes is often sparse or heterogeneous

HumanityAI's current P05 intervention evidence deliberately includes weak, null, and mixed findings:

- [`E0005`](../../data/evidence.json): evidence is insufficient to conclude that organised leisure-time sport improves risk behaviour, personal skills, or social-emotional outcomes for at-risk youth. The evidence base is sparse, non-randomized, and heterogeneous.
- [`E0010`](../../data/evidence.json): evidence is insufficient to determine whether arts interventions for children and young people at risk of offending improve offending, behavioural, psychosocial, or cognitive outcomes. Qualitative studies report valued positive experiences, but robust comparative effectiveness evidence is very limited.
- [`E0009`](../../data/evidence.json): summer employment programmes generally show limited or no effects on education and employment outcomes, while some summer education outcomes are positive.

The appropriate interpretation is **intervention-specific uncertainty**, not a general claim that extracurricular, arts, sport, or summer programmes do not matter. Some benefits may be intrinsic or valued by participants even when the evidence does not establish effects on the outcome families measured in reviews.

### Material conditions can affect education, but effects are not uniform

HumanityAI record [`E0001`](../../data/evidence.json) summarizes a Cochrane review of unconditional cash transfers in low- and middle-income countries. It reports probable or possible improvements in several welfare outcomes, including school attendance, while effects vary by outcome and much of the underlying evidence has high risk of bias.

This matters for P05 because barriers to human development are not confined to classroom design. Income insecurity, health, energy access, conflict, housing, discrimination, and infrastructure can change whether people are able to participate in and benefit from education. Cross-problem evidence should therefore be treated as causal context, not collapsed into one education score.

## What is not yet established

Current HumanityAI records do **not** establish:

- a single global measure of education or human development;
- a current global learning-quality baseline comparable across age groups and countries;
- which intervention family has the highest contemporary cost-effectiveness;
- that enrollment gains imply learning gains or long-term capability gains;
- that positive short-term test or attendance effects persist into later education, employment, health, civic participation, or wellbeing;
- how well existing reviews transfer across countries, cultures, languages, disability status, conflict settings, and different education systems;
- HumanityAI's comparative advantage in implementing, funding, or advocating any education programme.

## High-value next evidence

The strongest next work is complementary to the current access count and intervention reviews:

1. **Learning-quality baseline.** Add an authoritative global measure of minimum proficiency, learning poverty, literacy, numeracy, or another clearly defined outcome dimension, preserving age and comparability limits.
2. **Completion and persistence.** Distinguish enrollment from attendance, grade progression, completion, and dropout.
3. **Intervention heterogeneity.** Identify which design features, populations, implementation conditions, and contexts explain why early-education, summer, sport, arts, or cash-transfer effects differ.
4. **Long-term outcomes.** Prefer evidence that follows participants beyond immediate test scores or programme completion when available.
5. **Cost-effectiveness.** Preserve programme costs, implementation intensity, and opportunity costs rather than ranking interventions from effect direction alone.
6. **Affected groups.** Improve evidence for girls, disabled learners, displaced and conflict-affected children, linguistic minorities, rural populations, low-income adults, and people excluded from conventional schooling.
7. **Lifelong learning.** Current records are youth-heavy; adult literacy, reskilling, vocational learning, and access to scientific knowledge remain major gaps.
8. **Resource mapping.** P05 currently lacks a strong canonical map of datasets, standards, open educational infrastructure, and organizations that could support future work without duplicating existing systems.

## Rights and distribution cautions

More schooling is not automatically equivalent to more human capability. Education systems can reproduce exclusion, discrimination, coercion, inaccessible curricula, political indoctrination, unsafe environments, or credential barriers. Conversely, learning outside formal schools can have substantial value even when it is poorly captured by enrollment statistics.

P05 analysis should preserve learner agency, family and cultural pluralism, disability access, language, safety, privacy, unequal resource allocation, and the distribution of both benefits and burdens. Measures of achievement should not become a justification for narrowing education to whatever is easiest to test.
