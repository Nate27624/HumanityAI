# P11 — Climate, biodiversity, and environmental stability

**Status:** early synthesis, reviewed 2026-09-07  
**Problem definition:** climate change, pollution, ecosystem degradation, biodiversity loss, resource depletion, and environmental conditions that constrain present and future human freedom.

This brief summarizes current HumanityAI records plus a small amount of current authoritative context. It is not a priority ranking, a claim that climate risk is reducible to one metric, or a recommendation to implement any particular intervention.

## What the current evidence says

### The warming baseline remains historically extreme

HumanityAI indicator [`I0009`](../../data/indicators.json) records the World Meteorological Organization's consolidated estimate that **2025 global average surface temperature was 1.44 °C above the 1850–1900 average, with an uncertainty margin of ±0.13 °C**. WMO reports that 2025 was one of the three warmest years on record and that 2015–2025 were the 11 warmest years in the eight datasets it consolidated.

Source: World Meteorological Organization, *WMO confirms 2025 was one of warmest years on record* (14 January 2026): https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/wmo-confirms-2025-was-one-of-warmest-years-record

WMO's later *State of the Global Climate 2025* also reports continued ocean warming, very low sea-ice conditions, glacier loss, and disruptive extreme-weather impacts. Those are complementary dimensions of environmental instability; they should not be compressed into the annual surface-temperature anomaly.

Source: WMO, *State of the Global Climate 2025* (23 March 2026): https://wmo.int/publication-series/state-of-global-climate/state-of-global-climate-2025

### Near-term warming uncertainty is forecastable, but not resolved yet

HumanityAI prediction [`PR0001`](../../data/predictions.json) records an **86% probability** that at least one calendar year from 2026 through 2030 will be warmer than 2024 in WMO's consolidated annual global-temperature assessments. That probability was adopted directly from WMO's 28 May 2026 Global Annual-to-Decadal Climate Update rather than being independently invented by HumanityAI.

WMO also reports a 91% chance that at least one year in 2026–2030 will temporarily exceed 1.5 °C above the 1850–1900 average. A temporary annual exceedance is not equivalent to permanently crossing the Paris Agreement's long-term temperature threshold.

Source: WMO, *Global Annual to Decadal Climate Update 2026–2035* (28 May 2026): https://wmo.int/resources/publication-series/wmo-global-annual-decadal-climate-update/global-annual-decadal-climate-update-2026-2035

### Existing intervention evidence is already mixed

HumanityAI evidence record [`E0003`](../../data/evidence.json) summarizes a 2025 Campbell systematic review of 47 rigorous evaluations of off-grid electrification interventions in low- and middle-income countries. The review found reduced kerosene consumption, small income gains, and increased women's decision-making, but no statistically significant effect on school attendance, test scores, or air quality; only one included study measured CO2 emissions.

This is important for P11 because an intervention can be plausibly climate-relevant without having robust measured climate outcomes. Substituting technology adoption, energy access, or reduced kerosene use for demonstrated greenhouse-gas or health effects would overstate what the evidence establishes.

Source: Campbell Collaboration, *Improving Energy Access, Climate and Socio-Economic Outcomes Through Off-Grid Electrification Technologies: A Systematic Review* (14 October 2025): https://www.campbellcollaboration.org/review/improving-energy-access-climate-and-socio-economic-outcomes-through-off-grid-electrification-technologies-a-systematic-review/

## Additional intervention evidence worth integrating

The following systematic reviews are not yet canonical HumanityAI evidence records. They are included here as source-grounded context and should be independently checked before any later machine-readable integration.

### Climate-smart agriculture promotion: adoption signals, weak outcome certainty

A 2024 Campbell systematic review examined eight impact evaluations of interventions promoting climate-smart agricultural practices in low- and middle-income countries. All evaluated interventions were knowledge-dissemination approaches. Several studies reported improved knowledge or adoption of specific practices, but the review judged the evidence uncertain because of methodological limitations and major gaps in sex-disaggregated evidence, direct targeting of women farmers, financial incentives, and institutional interventions.

This distinction matters: **knowledge and adoption are intermediate outcomes, not proof of improved resilience, income, food security, emissions, or ecosystem outcomes**. The review supports cautious optimism about some dissemination approaches while leaving important final outcomes unresolved.

Source: Campbell Collaboration, *Interventions promoting resilience through climate smart agricultural practices for women farmers: A systematic review* (27 August 2024): https://www.campbellcollaboration.org/review/interventions-promoting-resilience-through-climate-smart-agricultural-practices-for-women-farmers-a-systematic-review/

### Agroforestry: positive yield signal, weak ecosystem and equity evidence

A 2021 Campbell systematic review synthesized 11 quasi-experimental evaluations covering 15 agroforestry programmes in low- and middle-income countries. It reported a large positive average effect on agricultural yields with substantial heterogeneity and a very small positive average income effect. All included studies had methodological weaknesses and high risk of bias; no randomized evaluations were identified.

Crucially for P11, the review found **too few impact evaluations of ecosystem services, nutrition, and food security to support strong conclusions**, even though broader ecological literature provides mechanisms for agroforestry benefits. It also reported mixed equity findings and context dependence: lower-fertility areas tended to show larger yield gains, while conservation-oriented interventions on already productive land could reduce yields.

Source: Campbell Collaboration, *The impacts of agroforestry interventions on agricultural productivity, ecosystem services, and human well-being in low- and middle-income countries* (1 June 2021): https://www.campbellcollaboration.org/review/agroforestry-in-low-and-middle-income-countries/

### Forest incentives and decentralization: effects can be modest and distributionally uneven

Older Campbell reviews remain useful as cautionary evidence, but their age should materially reduce confidence in using them as a current policy summary.

A 2014 review of payment-for-environmental-services programmes found only a modest average reduction in deforestation across the available quasi-experimental evidence, with methodological weaknesses and evidence that programmes could disproportionately attract wealthier landowners or perform worse in poorer areas. A separate 2014 review of decentralized forest management found modest average reductions in deforestation and some positive household-income evidence, but little evidence on poorer households and one study suggesting poorer households could lose income.

These reviews illustrate why P11 analysis must keep **environmental outcomes, household welfare, distribution, participation constraints, and cost-effectiveness separate** rather than assuming a conservation intervention automatically increases human capability.

Sources:

- Campbell Collaboration, *Effects of payment for environmental services (PES) on deforestation and poverty in low- and middle-income countries* (19 December 2014): https://www.campbellcollaboration.org/review/payment-for-environmental-services-deforestation-poverty/
- Campbell Collaboration, *Effects of decentralized forest management (DFM) on deforestation and poverty in low- and middle-income countries* (19 December 2014): https://www.campbellcollaboration.org/review/decentralised-forest-management-effects-on-poverty/

## What is not yet established

Current HumanityAI records do **not** establish:

- a single defensible scalar measure of climate, biodiversity, pollution, and ecosystem risk;
- the relative contribution of each P11 dimension to present versus future human capability loss;
- which mitigation or adaptation intervention family has the highest global marginal cost-effectiveness;
- that adoption of a climate-relevant technology or practice implies improved climate, ecological, health, or welfare outcomes;
- that a positive average intervention effect transfers across locations with different ecological, institutional, and economic conditions;
- the distributional effects of many environmental interventions on poorer households, Indigenous communities, workers, tenants, or people dependent on natural resources;
- HumanityAI's comparative advantage in implementing, funding, or advocating any climate or conservation intervention.

## High-value next evidence

The strongest next work is complementary rather than another temperature statistic:

1. **Biodiversity baseline.** Add an authoritative global biodiversity or ecosystem-condition measure with explicit methodological and aggregation limits.
2. **Pollution burden.** Add a complementary exposure or health-burden indicator without double-counting environmental harms already represented elsewhere.
3. **Intervention integration.** Independently verify and canonicalize recent systematic-review evidence on climate-smart agriculture, agroforestry, adaptation, conservation, and pollution control where outcome evidence is strong enough.
4. **Mitigation outcome evidence.** Prefer measured emissions, energy, health, ecosystem, or welfare outcomes over technology-installation or policy-adoption counts.
5. **Adaptation effectiveness.** Distinguish reduced hazard exposure, reduced vulnerability, improved resilience, and avoided losses; these are related but not interchangeable outcomes.
6. **Cost-effectiveness.** Record intervention costs and opportunity costs when credible comparative evidence exists rather than ranking by effect direction alone.
7. **Heterogeneity and distribution.** Preserve who benefits, who pays, land-tenure effects, local governance, gender, income, and baseline ecological conditions.
8. **Forecast calibration.** Resolve `PR0001` exactly as preregistered and add shorter-horizon, independently resolvable environmental forecasts so calibration does not depend on one multi-year temperature question.

## Rights and distribution cautions

Environmental stability can expand human freedom by protecting health, livelihoods, housing, food systems, and future options. Environmental policy can also restrict freedom or shift burdens when land use, energy access, housing costs, mobility, employment, or resource rights are changed without adequate consent or compensation.

P11 analysis should therefore preserve both environmental externalities and intervention externalities. Aggregate emissions reductions or ecosystem gains should not erase displacement, regressive costs, concentrated political power, weak procedural rights, or unequal access to benefits.
